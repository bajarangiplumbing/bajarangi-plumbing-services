This is the work I am called out for most, and the work most often made worse by guessing. Somebody opens the wall under the damp patch, finds dry pipe, and now you have a hole and a leak.

So I trace it. I isolate sections and watch what the meter does with everything closed. I check the obvious suspects first, in order of how often they are actually the cause: mixer bodies and their concealed tails, the connection behind a WC cistern, the shower diverter, waste joints, and only then buried supply runs. Where the leak is upstairs and showing downstairs, I work out whether it is a supply line under pressure or a waste run that only leaks when the fixture is used — those two behave completely differently and point to different pipes.

I am straight about the limits of doing this without specialist gear. I do not own thermal imaging or acoustic equipment. What I have is fifteen years of knowing where these pipes run in Bhubaneswar buildings and a methodical way of eliminating them. That solves the large majority of leaks with one small opening rather than several. If yours turns out to need instruments I do not have, I will tell you that instead of chasing it through your walls at your expense.

  • Isolate and observe

    Everything closed, then watch the meter and the wet area. A leak that continues with nothing running is on the pressurised side. One that only appears when a fixture is used is on the waste side. That single test rules out half the possibilities.

  • Eliminate the likely causes in order

    Fittings, seals and concealed valve bodies before buried pipe, because that is where leaks actually are most of the time. I check from the accessible side wherever there is one.

  • Open the smallest hole that will do

    When something has to be opened, I open it where the evidence says the leak is, not where the stain is. One tidy opening rather than exploratory damage across a wall.

  • Repair, pressure-test, then close up

    Repair the section properly rather than clamping over it, restore pressure and leave it under load while I watch the joint. Then I make good, and I tell you honestly how long the wall needs to dry before you repaint — painting over a damp wall is how people conclude the leak came back.

    Can you find a leak without breaking the wall?

    Often, yes. Isolating sections and watching the meter, plus checking every accessible fitting, locates most leaks without opening anything. When something does have to be opened, the point of tracing first is that it is one small hole in the right place.

    What I cannot do is promise zero opening on a buried run. Anyone who promises that before looking is guessing.

    Do you use thermal or acoustic leak detection?

    No, and I would rather say so than imply otherwise. I work by isolation, elimination and knowing how these buildings are piped. If your leak genuinely needs instruments, I will tell you after tracing rather than chase it through your walls.

    The damp keeps coming back after painting. Is that a leak?

    Usually, though not always — it can also be rising damp or a failed external seal. The difference matters, because a plumber cannot fix the second one. I will tell you which it looks like rather than sell you a repair for the wrong problem.

    My meter moves with all the taps closed. How bad is that?

    It means water is escaping somewhere on the pressurised side, and it will not improve on its own. Worth looking at soon, even if you cannot see any water yet — the ones you cannot see are the ones that damage the building.
